\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Much company news is published before the stock market opens. Two questions follow. Can a model that reads that
news anticipate how the price will react? And once part of the reaction is visible, can it tell whether the
market moved too far or not far enough? Both are old questions in empirical finance. Prices react to firm news
within minutes \citep{patell1984,busse2002}, media tone predicts short-horizon returns \citep{tetlock2007}, and
moves that come with news tend to drift while moves without news tend to reverse \citep{chan2003}. Large
language models (LLMs) now read news at scale, and several studies find that their readings predict returns
\citep{lopezlira2026,chen2022}. We ask whether such predictability survives a test built to rule out the usual
ways a backtest misleads: look-ahead in the data, memorization in the model and selection over many variants.

We study \emph{AI decision models}. A decision model is a non-generative model that takes a \emph{state} (text
plus structured context) and a set of typed questions (a choice among options, a score on an ordered scale, or
a yes-or-no question) and returns probabilities over the answers instead of generating text
\citep{zhu2026}. Decision models are close relatives of LLM-based classifiers and of probabilistic
forecasting with language models \citep{kadavath2022,halawi2024,schoenegger2024}, and their vendors describe the
probabilities as calibrated. They suit finance because they return the quantities a forecaster needs, such as
a distribution over the size of the coming move, at a cost of a few cents per thousand decisions, and because
open-weights versions can be fine-tuned.

Language models also bring a specific threat. A model trained on text published after the period on which it
is tested may know how events turned out, and even a model with an earlier training cutoff can recognize a
company from the content of its news \citep{glasserman2024,sarkar2024,lopezlira2025}. A backtest of a
language-model signal on historical news therefore mixes the model's ability to read news with its memory of
what followed. A second threat is older: when many signals, filters and horizons are tried, the best backtest
overstates what a strategy will earn \citep{harvey2016,bailey2014}. Pre-registration, fixing the analysis before
the data are seen, is the standard remedy in other sciences and is still rare in empirical finance
\citep{harvey2017,olken2015}.

We combine these elements in OLIVIA, a protocol for testing language-model signals on financial news without
look-ahead (Table~\ref{tab:olivia}), and apply it to the point-in-time S\&P 500 from July 2024 to July 2026
(Figure~\ref{fig:design}). For each company and trading session we build the news state as of 09:15~ET and mask
company names, tickers, people, dates and every mention of a price move. Before the open, each model gives a
\emph{pre-open reading}: how the stock will perform once the news is absorbed. At 10:00 and after the close it
gives a \emph{post-reaction reading}: was the reaction so far too small or too large for the news? The primary
instrument, Laya-Fin, is an open-weights decision model that we fine-tuned on 2024--2025 news and returns; we
compare it with three commercial decision models, the untuned Laya model and standard baselines. The hypotheses
and selection rules were written down before any data were analyzed, and every later change is dated in an
append-only log; selection used two validation quarters of 2025, and the analysis plan was frozen by hash before
the 2026 test outcomes were unblinded, once.

\needspace{8\baselineskip}
\paragraph{Key findings.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*), leftmargin=*, itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Pre-open news is priced at the open.} The commercial models' readings line up with the overnight
  move from the previous close to the open (daily information coefficient, IC, \res{d1-ic-on-test} to
  \res{drex-ic-on-test}) but not with the move after the open (about zero). No model predicts the move after the
  open, which is the part a trader who reads the news before the open can still earn. The untuned Laya model,
  which barely recognizes masked companies, shows the same split.
\item \textbf{A validated signal did not survive the blinded test.} Laya-Fin's IC with the day's move was
  \res{lf-ic-valb} ($t = \res{lf-ic-valb-t1}$) in the final validation quarter and \res{lf-ic-test}
  ($t = \res{lf-ic-test-t1}$) on the test. Every model whose validation IC was clearly positive fell, none of the five
  primary hypotheses is supported, and no pre-registered strategy survives costs and a deflated Sharpe ratio. In
  exploratory analyses, the signal that remained sat on earnings days, and conditions selected on two validation
  quarters, with in-sample Sharpe ratios up to \res{cb-valb-gross-max}, failed on the test.
\item \textbf{The models cannot judge the reaction.} In the pre-registered over/under regression the news
  coefficient is not positive (Laya-Fin $b = \res{lf-h2-b-d2}$, $p = \res{lf-h2-bp-d2}$), and the moves the
  models call underreactions never catch up. Out of sample, same-day news explains \res{lf-ladder-post-pct}
  (Laya-Fin) to \res{d1-ladder-post-pct} (d1-ultra) of a stock's own daily variance, and the commercial models'
  stated probabilities overstate their accuracy.
\item \textbf{Masking is not anonymity.} Given masked news, the commercial models pick the company out of four
  same-sector candidates in \res{mem-min} to \res{mem-max} of cases, against a matched baseline of
  \res{mem-baseline}; the open-weights Laya model does so in \res{mem-laya}.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Contributions.} First, we give OLIVIA, a protocol of six safeguards (one-time unblinding,
look-ahead-free states, identity masking, validation-only selection, an immutable plan and an audit trail), which
Table~\ref{tab:olivia} lists as a checklist that other studies can adopt. Second, we give a test of whether a
language-model news signal can be traded: splitting each day's move at the open shows that the predictable part of
pre-open news is already in the opening price. Third, we add two measurement tools for language models in finance:
a matched-distractor test of whether a model recognizes masked companies, and a firm-level version of the
explanation ladder of \citet{kelly2026}, which measures how much of a stock's own daily move its news explains.
